## 0. Version Control and Contradiction Scan

Record filename, cover date, page count, PDF metadata date, and visible version label before interpreting claims. If another deck or report uses a different version, do not merge conclusions silently.

Create an internal contradiction table for:

- text versus charts or footnotes;
- the same market, shipment, revenue, or product metric on different pages;
- currencies, units, exchange rates, CAGR, and arithmetic;
- actuals versus forecasts;
- pilot, paid deployment, order, shipment, revenue, collection, and profit;
- product availability versus roadmap;
- financing amount versus use-of-funds totals.

An inconsistency is itself a diligence finding. State the narrowest supported conclusion rather than choosing the more favorable number.

## 1. Claim and Evidence Model

For every material claim, capture:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Claim | Exact proposition being assessed |
| Source | BP page, filing, paper, official site, customer record |
| Status | Disclosed, verified, contradicted, inferred, unresolved |
| Scope | Product, geography, customer type, period |
| Metric definition | Numerator, denominator, unit, measurement conditions |
| Investment implication | Why the claim matters |
| Diligence evidence | What would confirm or reject it |

Common category errors:

- Current revenue versus future target
- Test deployment versus paid deployment
- Signed contract versus framework agreement or intention
- Image resolution versus ranging accuracy
- Academic benchmark versus field uptime
- Global residential market versus domestic commercial market
- Theoretical equipment replacement versus annual sales
- Electricity cost versus full lifecycle cost

## 2. Company and Product Analysis

Explain the company through four questions:

1. What customer job does it perform?
2. What product or service performs that job?
3. Who pays, how much, and how often?
4. Why can this company deliver better economics or outcomes?

For each product, capture:

- Status: concept, prototype, pilot, paid deployment, standard product, mass production
- Target customer and use case
- Selling price and business model
- Core configuration and dependencies
- Tested operating conditions
- Delivery, certification, maintenance, and warranty readiness
- Revenue, margin, repeat order, and return data when available

Flag overly broad portfolios. Platform narratives are credible only after one or more products demonstrate reusable capabilities, repeatable sales, and production reliability.

## 3. Market Validation

### Separate the market layers

- **Theoretical TAM:** complete replacement under management assumptions
- **Observed market:** actual annual sales under a named category
- **SAM:** customers the current product and channel can serve
- **SOM:** realistic share over a defined period

### Require market metadata

Every market number needs:

- currency and unit;
- year or forecast period;
- geography;
- residential/commercial or other segment scope;
- revenue, shipment, installed base, or replacement-value definition;
- source and publication date.

### Cross-validation method

1. Reproduce the BP calculation.
2. Find at least one external observed-market estimate.
3. Prefer a second independent source when definitions differ.
4. Explain why estimates diverge.
5. Build bottom-up SAM/SOM from target customers, units per site, price, replacement cycle, and win rate.

Do not average incompatible estimates. Present a range or a definition table instead.

## 4. Competition Analysis

### Segment the competitive landscape first

If the company serves more than one market, build a separate competitor set for every material customer segment or use case. For example, do not combine residential, commercial, golf-course, industrial-slope, and component-platform competitors in one ranking merely because they share a technology label.

For each segment, state:

- the company product and target buyer;
- the buyer's decision criteria;
- direct competitors with the same customer and job;
- adjacent entrants with relevant technology, channel, or installed base;
- substitutes such as labor, outsourced service, conventional equipment, or an alternative workflow;
- the company's evidence-backed relative position and recommended strategic role.

End with a segment summary that compares competition intensity, evidence maturity, and recommended focus. A company may be weak in a crowded horizontal market but differentiated in a narrow vertical one.

Separate competitors into:

- Direct: same customer, use case, product class, and price band
- Adjacent: can enter through existing technology, channel, or installed base
- Substitute: manual labor, outsourced service, conventional equipment, alternative workflow
- Financial comparable: useful for margins or valuation but not a direct competitor

Compare on decision-relevant dimensions:

- Customer segment and geography
- Product maturity and deployment scale
- Navigation or core technology route
- Terrain/environment capability
- Reliability, certification, and serviceability
- Price and unit economics
- Channel, brand, and service network
- Fleet management, data, and recurring revenue

Challenge “only,” “first,” “leading,” and “no direct competitor” claims through official competitor pages and patents or papers when relevant.

### Competitor timeline

For every “first,” “only,” or open-window claim, build a timeline with product launch, demonstrated prototype, mass production, price, shipments/deployments, and channel scale. Use the narrowest competitor set that actually matches the claim. A broad incumbent list is not a substitute for direct product-route competitors.

When comparing scale targets, record how long peers took to reach the target and what assets they started with: installed base, channel, capital, brand, contract manufacturing, certifications, or service network.

## 5. Technology Validation

Describe the technology stack before assessing it:

- sensing and perception;
- localization and mapping;
- planning and control;
- compute, software platform, and cloud;
- mechanical/electrical system;
- data and operational feedback loop.

### Evidence ladder

1. Management assertion
2. Demo or image
3. Internal test with conditions
4. Peer-reviewed method
5. Third-party benchmark
6. Paid field deployment
7. Long-duration reliability and unit economics

A higher step does not automatically validate a different claim. A paper validates a method, not mass-production reliability.

### Technology leadership cross-check

For each leadership claim, report:

- **Supported:** what external evidence confirms
- **Qualified:** what is directionally true but narrowly defined
- **Unsupported:** what lacks comparable metrics or independent data
- **Contradicted:** what competitor or paper evidence disproves
- **Latest development:** where the frontier is moving

Useful production metrics include latency, frame rate, power, temperature, accuracy by condition, intervention rate, task completion, uptime, MTBF, return rate, safety events, OTA success, and maintenance hours.

## 6. Commercialization and Unit Economics

Traction hierarchy:

1. Evaluation or free pilot
2. Paid pilot
3. Initial order
4. Repeat order or expansion
5. Standard deployment across customers
6. Positive contribution margin and cash collection

Rebuild full lifecycle economics. Include:

- selling price or service revenue;
- BOM and manufacturing labor;
- channel margin;
- transport and deployment;
- operator labor;
- consumables and batteries;
- maintenance, warranty, and field support;
- depreciation and financing cost;
- utilization and downtime;
- collection period and bad-debt risk.

Reject electricity-only cost comparisons presented as total operating cost.

### Model each business model separately

Do not combine economics for materially different revenue models:

- **Device sale:** net ASP less BOM, manufacturing labor, channel, logistics, installation, customization, expected warranty, and returns.
- **Lease:** recurring revenue less depreciation, financing, maintenance, insurance, downtime, retrieval, refurbishment, and residual-value risk.
- **Per-task / per-unit / RaaS:** price per output less all fleet operating costs, including utilization and local service overhead.
- **Software or platform:** license/subscription revenue less cloud, integration, customer success, version support, and third-party royalties.
- **Components:** component ASP less BOM, test/yield loss, manufacturing, channel, technical support, and warranty.

### Reconcile customer ROI and company economics

Calculate them separately:

- Customer ROI: avoided incumbent cost, implementation cost, retained labor, productivity, quality, safety, downtime, and payback period.
- Company economics: revenue, gross margin, contribution margin, working capital, cash collection, warranty tail, and corporate overhead.

A large customer saving does not prove company profitability, and a high product gross margin does not prove positive cash economics.

### Use a full lifecycle formula

For output-based models, use:

`full unit cost = (depreciation + financing + labor + maintenance + consumables + batteries + transport/deployment + connectivity/software + insurance + field support + warranty) / accepted output + marginal energy cost`

Use accepted, billable output after weather, charging, setup, transit, intervention, fault, and idle time. Do not use laboratory throughput as annual utilization without reconciliation.

### Sensitivity and evidence rules

When company actuals are missing:

1. State that actual unit economics cannot be determined.
2. Build low/base/high scenarios only if they clarify the decision.
3. Label every assumed value and never describe it as company data.
4. Show units, formulas, and intermediate arithmetic.
5. Vary the most consequential inputs: utilization, ASP/price, device life, labor ratio, maintenance/warranty, downtime, channel take, collection, and financing.
6. Report break-even price or utilization where possible.
7. Identify the minimum evidence that would replace the assumptions.

Do not call contribution margin “gross margin” or cash payback “accounting profit.” State whether depreciation and corporate overhead are included.

## 7. Team, Entity, and Background Verification

Verify only decision-relevant claims:

- founder degrees and prior employment;
- academic titles and institutional roles;
- named awards, competition results, patents, and papers;
- founder-to-core-IP relationship;
- full-time status, invention assignment, and conflicts;
- legal names, jurisdictions, registration dates, shareholders, and related parties;
- similarly named companies or brands that could cause mistaken attribution;
- allocation of IP, people, contracts, revenue, and costs across entities.

Prefer university, employer, competition organizer, registry, regulator, patent office, and paper records. Treat people-search sites, business aggregators, and media profiles as leads rather than conclusive proof. Shared names do not establish identity or affiliation.

## 8. Forecast Ramp Stress Test

For every material step-change, calculate the required growth multiple and test:

- signed orders and conversion assumptions;
- channel count and productivity;
- certification timing;
- factory capacity, yield, suppliers, and lead times;
- installation and after-sales staffing;
- inventory and receivables working capital;
- warranty, return, safety, and service costs;
- peer time-to-scale under comparable starting conditions.

Do not infer accounting causes such as capitalization, subsidy dependence, or channel stuffing without evidence. Convert them into alternative hypotheses and evidence requests.

## 9. Financing and Investment Conclusion

Assess team-market fit, operating completeness, founder commitment, IP ownership, related parties, governance, and hiring gaps.

For financing, capture amount, valuation, dilution, historical rounds, runway, milestone allocation, and working-capital needs. Link use of funds to measurable milestones rather than generic percentages.

Default conclusion levels:

- Pass
- Monitor
- Proceed to diligence
- Proceed subject to named evidence
- Ready for term-sheet discussion

State the evidence that would change the conclusion.
